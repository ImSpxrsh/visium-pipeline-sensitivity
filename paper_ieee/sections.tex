\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

There is rarely one correct way to analyze a spatial transcriptomics
dataset. Going from raw counts to a map of spatial domains requires
choosing a normalization, a number of highly variable genes (HVGs), a number
of principal components, a $k$-nearest-neighbor or radius-based graph,
Leiden~\cite{traag2019louvain} or Louvain~\cite{blondel2008fast} clustering
and a resolution, and a test for spatially variable genes
(SVGs)~\cite{moran1950notes, svensson2018spatialde, weber2023nnsvg}. Each
option has been used in published work, so each is defensible. But a study
usually runs one pipeline and reports its output.

Gelman and Loken call this the \emph{garden of forking
paths}~\cite{gelman2013garden}: even without deliberate cherry-picking, a
reported result can depend on which path was taken rather than on the
biology. \emph{Multiverse analysis}~\cite{steegen2016increasing} and
\emph{specification-curve analysis}~\cite{simonsohn2020specification}
address this by running all reasonable analyses and reporting the spread of
outcomes. The problem is real: when 70 teams analyzed the same neuroimaging
dataset, they reached substantially different
conclusions~\cite{botvinik2020variability}. Existing spatial
transcriptomics benchmarks compare methods against a reference, but we are
not aware of work that measures how much an analyst's reasonable choices
change the results on a fixed tissue section.

We apply specification-curve analysis to spatial transcriptomics. This
paper makes four contributions:

\begin{enumerate}
\item[\textbf{C1}] A pipeline in which every analysis choice is a
  configuration setting, and the \emph{Spatial Stability Score} ($S^3$), which
  measures agreement across pipelines per spot, per gene, and for summary
  statistics, using Hungarian matching to align cluster labels
  (\S\ref{sec:method}).
\item[\textbf{C2}] Measurements of instability on two mouse-brain sections
  and one human tumor section, with permutation tests, bootstrap
  confidence intervals, and a variance decomposition across analysis
  choices (\S\ref{sec:results}).
\item[\textbf{C3}] A comparison against independent pathology annotations,
  showing that stability is lowest in the annotated region surrounding the
  tumor, including after controlling for sequencing depth
  (\S\ref{sec:tumor}).
\item[\textbf{C4}] Two practical uses of the pipeline collection: a
  co-association consensus that matches the best single pipeline without
  needing reference labels to pick it, and a classifier that predicts
  unstable spots from a single pipeline run
  (\S\ref{sec:consensus}--\S\ref{sec:downstream}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\textbf{Multiverse and specification-curve analysis.}
Steegen~\emph{et al.}~\cite{steegen2016increasing} introduced multiverse
analysis and Simonsohn~\emph{et al.}~\cite{simonsohn2020specification}
specification curves, both in psychology. Gelman and
Loken~\cite{gelman2013garden} explained why analytic flexibility is a
problem even without deliberate $p$-hacking, and
Botvinik-Nezer~\emph{et al.}~\cite{botvinik2020variability} showed its
effect across 70 fMRI analysis teams. None of these address spatial omics
data or the problem that cluster labels are arbitrary and must be aligned
before they can be compared.

\textbf{Spatial transcriptomics pipelines.} Standard analyses use
SCANPY~\cite{wolf2018scanpy} and Squidpy~\cite{palla2022squidpy}, with
Louvain~\cite{blondel2008fast} or
Leiden~\cite{traag2019louvain,traag2011narrow} clustering and SVG detection
by Moran's $I$~\cite{moran1950notes},
SpatialDE~\cite{svensson2018spatialde}, or nnSVG~\cite{weber2023nnsvg}.
Reference atlases such as the DLPFC dataset~\cite{maynard2021transcriptome}
are used for benchmarking, and reviews~\cite{luecken2019current} compare
methods against such references. Multi-platform integration tools such as
MOSAIK~\cite{baptista2025mosaik} combine data sources but do not measure analytical instability.
We ask a different question: with the data fixed, how much do the
results change across reasonable analysis choices, and where in the tissue
do they change most?

\textbf{Consensus clustering.} Our co-association consensus
(\S\ref{sec:consensus}) follows classical consensus
clustering~\cite{monti2003consensus} and cluster
ensembles~\cite{strehl2002cluster}, which usually combine partitions from
one method under resampling or different random initializations. We instead
combine partitions from pipelines that differ in normalization, features,
graph, and clustering. We show on real tissue that this consensus matches or
exceeds the best single pipeline's agreement with the reference without
using the reference to choose, and that per-spot $S^3$ indicates where the
consensus is likely wrong.

\textbf{Concurrent work on per-spot instability.} Wu
\emph{et al.}~\cite{wu2026instability} independently developed InSTability,
which measures per-spot disagreement across seven clustering methods
(BayesSpace, GraphST, Leiden, Louvain, SEDR, STAGATE, SpiceMix) on 18
benchmark samples, focusing on variation from repeated runs and choice of
method. We vary the earlier analysis steps as well (normalization,
feature selection, graph construction, resolution, and SVG test) and add a
consensus estimator and a single-pipeline predictor. The two measures
answer different questions: how much a result changes when the same method
is rerun, versus how much it changes under a different reasonable choice.

\textbf{Related work in other domains.} Similar pipeline dependence appears
elsewhere. Zhou and Roy~\cite{zhou2026matched} find that for EEG foundation
models on motor imagery, which architecture performs better depends on the
input pipeline. Roy \emph{et al.}~\cite{roy2026kaisen} find that the effect
of a fairness mitigation step in clinical risk models varies more across
cohort samples than across diseases, and recommend reporting the spread
across runs. We take the same approach for spatial transcriptomics.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Specification space}
Each analysis stage is an axis (Table~\ref{tab:space}). The full Cartesian
product has more than 200 combinations. Following standard
specification-curve practice, we evaluate a curated set of \Nspecs{}
pipelines covering typical, conservative, aggressive, and unusual but
defensible settings, which keeps the results small enough to inspect
directly.

\subsection{Pipeline}
Given a specification $s$, the pipeline turns raw counts into a clustered
\texttt{AnnData}~\cite{virshup2023anndata} object and an SVG table. All
random steps use a fixed seed (\texttt{random\_state}=42), and each
specification starts again from the raw counts, so specifications do not
share intermediate results. Running the same specification twice gives
identical spot labels.

\subsection{The Spatial Stability Score}
\label{sec:s3}
Let $\mathcal{S}$ be the set of specifications, $N=|\mathcal{S}|$, and
$c_s(i)\in\{1,\dots,K_s\}$ the cluster label of spot $i$ under $s$. Because
cluster labels are assigned independently in each specification, $c_s$ and
$c_{s'}$ cannot be compared directly. We align each specification to a
reference specification $s_0$ using the overlap matrix
$M^{(s)}_{ab}=|\{i: c_{s_0}(i)=a \wedge c_s(i)=b\}|$ and find the matching
that maximizes overlap,
\begin{equation}
  \pi_s = \arg\max_{\pi} \sum_{b=1}^{K_s} M^{(s)}_{\pi(b),b},
\end{equation}
with the Hungarian algorithm~\cite{kuhn1955hungarian} ($O(K^3)$,
$K=\max(K_{s_0},K_s)$). With aligned labels $\tilde c_s(i)=\pi_s(c_s(i))$,
per-spot stability is the fraction of specifications that give the most
common label,
\begin{equation}
  S^3_{\text{spot}}(i) = \frac{1}{N}\max_{\ell}\left|\{s\in\mathcal{S}:\tilde
  c_s(i)=\ell\}\right| \in \left[\tfrac1N,1\right].
\end{equation}
Per-gene stability is the fraction of specifications that call gene $g$ a
significant SVG,
\begin{equation}
  S^3_{\text{gene}}(g) = \frac1N\sum_{s\in\mathcal{S}} \mathbb{1}[g\in\text{SVG}_s].
  \label{eq:s3gene}
\end{equation}
For summary statistics such as the number of domains, we report the mean,
standard deviation, and range across $\mathcal{S}$. We also report mean
pairwise Adjusted Rand Index (ARI)~\cite{hubert1985comparing} between
specifications, which does not need label alignment.

\textbf{Sensitivity to the reference specification.} $S^3_{\text{spot}}$
depends on the choice of $s_0$. To check how much, we recomputed it with
each of the \RefRobustN{} mouse-brain specifications as the reference. The
mean $S^3$ barely changes: it falls in $[\RefRobustAggMeanRange{}]$ across
references (standard deviation \RefRobustAggMeanStd{}). Individual spots
change more. The mean pairwise Pearson correlation between per-spot $S^3$
vectors from different references is \RefRobustMeanPearson{} (lowest pair
\RefRobustMinPearson{}), and a spot's $S^3$ has a standard deviation of
\RefRobustMeanPerSpotStd{} across references on average. We therefore
interpret $S^3_{\text{spot}}$ at the level of regions or the whole section
and treat single-spot values as approximate.

\subsection{Statistical tests}
\label{sec:stats}
Each positive result is reported with a null test and a confidence interval.
\textbf{Permutation tests} keep per-spot scores fixed and shuffle region or
boundary labels ($B{=}1000$, preserving group sizes), with a one-sided
empirical $p$-value using add-one correction. \textbf{Depth-stratified
permutation tests} first bin spots by sequencing-depth decile and shuffle
labels only within each bin, so the null keeps each group's depth
distribution. This tests directly whether an effect is explained by
sequencing depth. \textbf{Bootstrap confidence intervals} resample spots
with replacement ($B{=}1000$) to give 95\% CIs. \textbf{Variance
decomposition} fits a Type-II ANOVA of a summary statistic on all analysis
axes to estimate each axis's share of the variance. Finally, we
\textbf{correlate $S^3$ with sequencing depth} to check for a technical
confound.

\subsection{Computational cost}
With $S$ spots, $H$ retained genes, $d$ PCA components, $P$ SVG
permutations, and $N$ specifications, each specification costs
$O(SHd + PSH)$, mostly from SVG permutation testing, so a full sweep costs
$O(N(SHd+PSH))$. Label alignment adds $O(NK^3)$, which is small since
$K\ll S$. Specifications are independent and can run in parallel. Results
are cached by a hash of the configuration, so rerunning a sweep only
computes new specifications.

\section{Experimental Setup}
We use two 10x Genomics Visium mouse-brain coronal sections distributed with
Squidpy~\cite{palla2022squidpy}: an H\&E section (\Nspots{} spots, \Ngenes{}
genes) as the main dataset, and a separately prepared fluorescence section
(2{,}800 spots) to check generalization. Both come with histology images and
a 15-region anatomical annotation, which we use as the reference. We also
run all pipelines on the public 10x Visium human breast-cancer section
(\texttt{V1\_Breast\_Cancer\_Block\_A\_Section\_1}; invasive ductal
carcinoma; 3{,}798 spots), with a single shared gene filter so that all
specifications use the same genes. For this section we use per-spot
pathology annotations (Invasive, Tumor, Surrounding tumor, Healthy)
released with the SEDR study~\cite{fu2021sedr}, matched to Visium barcodes.
These labels were made independently of every pipeline we evaluate. All
datasets are public and downloaded by script; we generated no new data.

\section{Results: Mouse Brain}
\label{sec:results}

\textbf{Number of domains.} The number of spatial domains ranges from
\ClustMin{} to \ClustMax{} across pipelines (mean
$\ClustMean{}\pm\ClustStd{}$). Mean pairwise ARI between specifications is
\AriMean{} (range $\AriMin{}$--$\AriMax{}$), so the pipelines produce
different partitions, not just relabeled copies of one partition.

\textbf{Instability at boundaries.} \SpotStableFrac{} of spots are stable
($S^3\ge0.8$) and \SpotFragileFrac{} are unstable ($S^3<0.5$). Mean $S^3$ is
\BoundaryS{} for spots on region boundaries and \InteriorS{} for interior
spots, a gap of \StakesGap{} ($p\,\StakesP{}$, permutation test).

\textbf{Relation to anatomy and sequencing depth.} Individual
specifications agree with the reference anatomy at mean ARI \RefAriMean{}
(best \RefAriMax{}). In a permutation test,
\RegionsStable{}/\NregionsTotal{} anatomical regions are more stable than
chance ($p<0.05$). $S^3$ is nearly uncorrelated with sequencing depth
($r=\ConfoundR{}$), so on this section instability is not explained by
low coverage. (The consensus in \S\ref{sec:consensus} improves on the
per-specification ARI; here we only show that individual pipelines
recover meaningful anatomy.)

\textbf{Clustering resolution dominates.} A Type-II ANOVA attributes
\VarTopFrac{} of the variance in domain count to clustering \VarTopAxis{},
with every other axis contributing at most a few percent
(Fig.~\ref{fig:variance}). Resolution is therefore the most important
setting to fix and report.

\textbf{Gene-level variation.} Per-gene $S^3$ (Eq.~\ref{eq:s3gene}) is a
selection frequency, so a gene that is consistently \emph{not} called an
SVG scores near 0, the same as a gene that switches back and forth. By this
measure \GeneSelFreqLowFrac{} of genes have $S^3<0.5$, but this mostly
reflects genes that are never called spatially variable, not unstable
calls (\GeneRobustFrac{} are consistently significant, $S^3>0.8$). To
separate the two cases we also compute call consistency,
$\max(p_g,1-p_g)$ where $p_g$ is the selection frequency. By this measure
\GeneConsistentFrac{} of genes are called consistently, and the unstable
calls are limited to a smaller set of genes near the significance
threshold.

\textbf{Effect on downstream models.} A cross-validated classifier that
predicts anatomical region from each specification's embedding varies by
\DownstreamRangePP{} percentage points in accuracy
($\DownstreamAccMin{}$--$\DownstreamAccMax{}$) depending only on the
upstream pipeline. Its multiclass Brier score varies more
($\BrierMin{}$--$\BrierMax{}$), so pipeline choice affects calibration
more than accuracy (Fig.~\ref{fig:downstreamml}).

\textbf{Second section.} The fluorescence section shows a similar $S^3$
distribution (mostly stable spots with a tail of unstable ones) and similar
pairwise ARI, so the pattern is not specific to one sample.

\section{Results: Human Breast Cancer}
\label{sec:tumor}

Instability is somewhat larger on tumor tissue: mean $S^3$ is
\TumorBoundaryS{} at consensus-domain boundaries and \TumorInteriorS{} in
interiors (gap \TumorGap{}, $p\,\TumorStakesP{}$), compared with a gap of
\StakesGap{} in brain. Unlike in brain, $S^3$ is correlated with sequencing
depth here ($r=\TumorConfoundR{}$ versus $\ConfoundR{}$), so we also run the
depth-stratified permutation test from \S\ref{sec:stats}. The gap remains
significant ($p\,\TumorDepthP{}$), so it is not explained by low-coverage
spots alone.

\textbf{Comparison with pathology annotations.} Instead of boundaries
derived from our own consensus domains, we can use the SEDR
annotations~\cite{fu2021sedr} (Invasive, Tumor [in situ], Surrounding
tumor, Healthy). Stability is lowest in the ``Surrounding tumor'' region:
mean $S^3$ is \PathMarginS{} there, versus \PathInvasiveS{} in the invasive
region and \PathHealthyS{} in healthy tissue ($p\,\PathP{}$;
Fig.~\ref{fig:pathology}). We report bootstrap CIs ($B{=}1000$, resampling
spots) for two comparisons. The surrounding-tumor versus invasive gap is
\PathMarginInvasiveGap{} (95\% CI
$[\PathMarginInvasiveCILow{}, \PathMarginInvasiveCIHigh{}]$), and the
surrounding-tumor versus all-other-tissue gap is \PathMarginRestGap{} (95\%
CI $[\PathMarginRestCILow{}, \PathMarginRestCIHigh{}]$). Both exclude zero
and both remain significant under the depth-stratified test
($p\,\PathDepthP{}$). Note that ``Surrounding tumor'' is the dataset's
pathology label, not a verified surgical margin. It is the tissue next to
the invasive region, which is relevant to margin assessment, but we do not
make clinical claims from one section.

The most important analysis axis is the same as in brain: clustering
resolution explains the most variance in domain count (\TumorVarTopFrac{},
versus \VarTopFrac{} in brain). We had hypothesized that the dominant axis
would differ by tissue type; this hypothesis was not supported.

\section{Consensus Across Pipelines}
\label{sec:consensus}

Besides measuring instability, the set of pipelines can be combined. We
build a co-association matrix (the fraction of specifications that put each
pair of spots in the same aligned cluster) and cluster it to get consensus
domains. Single pipelines agree with the reference anatomy at ARI
\SingleWorstARI{}--\SingleBestARI{} (median \SingleMedianARI{}). The
consensus reaches \ConsensusARI{}, which matches or exceeds the best single
pipeline near the reference number of domains and beats the median pipeline
in \ConsensusBeatsMedianPct{} of the granularity and linkage settings we
tried (Fig.~\ref{fig:consensus}). This matters because in practice the
best single pipeline can only be identified using the reference labels,
which a researcher normally does not have. Per-spot $S^3$ also indicates
where the consensus is wrong: spots where the consensus disagrees with the
reference have lower $S^3$ (mean $0.69$ vs.\ $0.81$; Mann--Whitney
$p<10^{-40}$), and $S^3$ alone predicts whether the consensus is correct
at ROC AUC \ConsensusUncAUC{} (consensus accuracy \ConsensusAcc{}).

Fig.~\ref{fig:consensus} sweeps the number of clusters $k$ and the linkage
method against the reference, which shows the best achievable result.
Without a reference, a researcher still has to pick one setting. We
therefore choose $(k, \text{linkage})$ using only the co-association
matrix, by maximizing mean silhouette width on the co-association distance.
On mouse brain this picks \ConsensusBlindLinkage{} linkage with
$k=\ConsensusBlindK{}$, giving ARI \ConsensusBlindARI{}, the same as the
best setting chosen using the reference (ARI \ConsensusOracleARI{}, gap
\ConsensusBlindOracleGap{}). Across the full grid, silhouette width and
reference ARI have Spearman $\rho=\ConsensusSilARISpearman{}$. On this
dataset, then, silhouette width is a reasonable way to choose the consensus
settings without the reference.

\section{Predicting Unstable Spots from One Pipeline}
\label{sec:downstream}

Running all \Nspecs{} pipelines is not always practical, so we tested
whether unstable spots can be predicted from a single run. A
cross-validated logistic regression uses four features from one pipeline:
local transcriptomic heterogeneity, disagreement with neighboring spots,
sequencing depth, and number of genes detected. It predicts which spots
will be unstable across all pipelines ($S^3<0.5$) at ROC AUC \FragPredAUC{}
with random spot-level cross-validation (95\% CI
$[\FragPredAUCRandomCILow{}, \FragPredAUCRandomCIHigh{}]$, permutation
\FragPredPermP{}; Fig.~\ref{fig:fragpred}).

Random splits put neighboring spots, which share local structure, in both
training and test sets, so this estimate is optimistic. We therefore also
group spots into \FragPredSpatialBlocks{} spatial blocks (k-means on
coordinates) and hold out one block at a time. AUC drops to
\FragPredAUCSpatial{} (95\% CI
$[\FragPredAUCSpatialCILow{}, \FragPredAUCSpatialCIHigh{}]$, permutation
\FragPredPermP{}). Spots on either side of a block boundary can still be
neighbors, so we add a buffer: training spots within
\FragPredBufferMultiple{} times the median nearest-neighbor spacing of any
held-out spot are removed from that fold (\FragPredBufferDroppedPerFold{}
spots per fold on average). AUC falls further to \FragPredAUCBuffered{}
(95\% CI $[\FragPredAUCBufferedCILow{}, \FragPredAUCBufferedCIHigh{}]$,
permutation \FragPredPermP{}). The estimate decreases as more spatial
leakage is removed (\FragPredAUC{} $\to$ \FragPredAUCSpatial{} $\to$
\FragPredAUCBuffered{}) but stays above chance in every case. The best
single feature is disagreement with neighboring spots (AUC $0.77$), and
depth alone gives AUC $0.57$, so the model is not just picking up
low-coverage spots. This gives a rough, cheap indication of which parts of
a single analysis are least reliable, which can help decide whether the
full set of pipelines is worth running.

\section{Recommendations}
For researchers who cannot run many pipelines, the variance decomposition
(Fig.~\ref{fig:variance}) suggests an order of priority. First, choose and
report the clustering resolution, which accounts for \VarTopFrac{} of the
variation in domain count. Second, report the neighborhood size $k$.
Normalization, feature-selection method, number of HVGs, number of PCs, and
Leiden versus Louvain each had small effects in our data. SVG calls near
the significance threshold should be treated as tentative, and a per-gene
$S^3$ can be reported alongside an SVG list.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
Our approach measures analytic ambiguity rather than resolving it: it shows which
results hold across pipelines and which depend on specific choices, and the
consensus gives one way to combine them. On brain tissue, $S^3$ follows
anatomical boundaries and is not correlated with depth, which suggests it
reflects genuine ambiguity in the tissue (for example, transitions between
regions) rather than data quality.

This study has several limitations. The specification space is curated,
not exhaustive. We analyze three Visium sections (two mouse brain, one
human tumor), and results on other tissues, patients, and higher-resolution
platforms such as Xenium are untested. Where pathology annotations are not
available we use boundaries of our own consensus domains as a proxy. On
tumor tissue, the depth-stratified test controls for sequencing depth but
not for every possible technical covariate. The consensus results come from
mouse brain, where a reference annotation exists, and the silhouette-based
selection rule has only been checked there.

\textbf{Future work.} The \Nspecs{} specifications are a small sample of
the possible space. Adaptive methods such as Bayesian optimization could
choose which specifications to run next based on where $S^3$ is most
uncertain. Another option is to run the full set of pipelines only on
regions that the single-pipeline predictor (\S\ref{sec:downstream}) flags
as likely unstable, similar to the compute-allocation approach
in~\cite{roy2026belief}.

\section{Conclusion}
We ran many reasonable analysis pipelines on the same spatial
transcriptomics data and measured where their results agree. On mouse
brain, most of the disagreement comes from clustering resolution and is
concentrated at region boundaries. On a breast-cancer section, the least
stable region is the tissue annotated as surrounding the tumor. A consensus
across pipelines matches the best single pipeline without needing reference
labels to choose it, and unstable spots can be partly predicted from a
single run.

\section*{Reproducibility}
Every specification is seeded and starts from raw counts. Sweeps are cached
by configuration hash, and repeated runs of a fixed specification give
identical spot labels. Code, configuration, aggregate stability tables,
statistical outputs, and figure scripts are at
\url{https://github.com/ImSpxrsh/MOSAIC-ISEF}. Analyses use
NumPy~\cite{harris2020array}, SciPy~\cite{virtanen2020scipy},
scikit-learn~\cite{pedregosa2011scikit}, SCANPY~\cite{wolf2018scanpy}, and
Squidpy~\cite{palla2022squidpy}.
